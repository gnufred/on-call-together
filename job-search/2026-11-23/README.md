# Find your next IT job in France

**23 November 2026**

Open one job you would take. Underline the two things the team needs someone to do. Under each, write one sentence about a time you did similar work and how you checked the result. You now have the start of an application and two interview stories. If you want to work in English, check the team’s working language as early as the office schedule.

## Decide which job deserves an application

Pick the work you want to do again and search for it under two titles. Try `application support engineer` and `technical support engineer` for troubleshooting, `Linux production engineer` and `site reliability engineer` for operations, or `backend engineer` and `API developer` for API work. Set the location to France and try adding `English-speaking` to one search. Include employers outside tech. A retailer running an ordering system may need the same skills as the company that sells it. [France Travail vacancies](https://candidat.francetravail.fr/rechercheoffre/emploi) · [Apec professional and managerial vacancies](https://www.apec.fr/candidat/recherche-emploi.html) · [More search terms](search.md#search-by-the-work-then-by-title)

Open the employer’s posting or the recruiting agency’s original listing. Make one decision.

- **Apply** if you can show how you would handle the core work and the conditions fit. If Python is listed as *preferred* and you automated the same task in Bash, describe the Bash example and apply.
- **Ask** if one missing fact would change your decision. For “remote Europe,” try “Can I work from France in this role? Which office would I need to visit, how often, and is there any other business travel?” For language, ask “Can I interview in English, and what language does the team use during regular work and on-call?” [Check the team’s working language](search.md#check-the-teams-working-language)
- **Move on** if a confirmed condition rules it out. If several Paris roles require a commute you cannot make, save a France-wide remote search and check which employers can hire you where you live.

## Put one piece of work near the top of your CV

Suppose an ad asks for someone who can diagnose incidents and keep the support desk informed. This fictional example shows how to turn a half-remembered ticket into a CV bullet.

**What you remember.** “An order import stopped. I compared the last good file with the failed one and found a renamed CSV column. After the fix, I checked the row count in staging and sent the recovery steps to support.”

**What goes on the CV.** *Traced a failed order import to a renamed CSV column, verified that every expected row loaded in staging after the fix, and gave the support desk the recovery steps.*

Use your own incident, feature or handover. If you’re looking for your first role, a course, volunteer project or home lab works too. Name the setting and give the reviewer a test or example they can inspect. If the employer recruits in English, send your English CV in the requested format. Put your actual language levels on it, and save the posting, date and version you sent. [CV and portfolio guidance](applications.md)

## Use AI to challenge your application

Give an assistant the public ad and a few anonymised notes about your work. Try this prompt.

> Quote the two duties this posting emphasises most. Which sentence in my notes supports each one? Flag any claim you cannot substantiate. Ask me one question a hiring manager would ask next.

Check its answer against your notes. For the import example, a good interview question is *“How did you check that retrying the batch wouldn’t duplicate orders?”* Practise answering aloud. If you used AI in the work, explain one suggestion you accepted or rejected and how you tested it. Check the employer’s rules before using AI during an assessment. [Assessment policies and examples](applications.md#check-the-assessment-rules-for-each-stage)

Let the job description guide your learning. If several jobs you want involve evaluating model outputs, try routing a set of invented support tickets, compare the model’s answers with your own labels, and count how many urgent tickets went to the wrong queue. Explain which cases you would send to a person. If the work is Linux troubleshooting, spend that afternoon rehearsing a diagnosis you actually made.

## Ask one person a question they can answer

A former teammate may know what an ad leaves out. Try “Hi Camille, I saw the application support opening on your team. Are most incidents about data feeds or end-user devices? I’ve troubleshot failed order imports and would love to know how close that is to the day-to-day work.”

If you don’t know anyone there, give a peer the ad and the top half of your CV. Ask, *“Which sentence would make you invite me to interview? What would you ask me to explain?”* Use the answer to edit one bullet or practise one story. [30-minute onCallTogether session](fieldwork.md#run-a-30-minute-oncalltogether-session)

## Change the step where you’re getting stuck

At the end of the week, review applications that have had time to receive a response. Pick one change to test on the next comparable role.

| What keeps happening | What to do next |
| --- | --- |
| Suitable applications rarely lead to calls. | Ask a peer to find evidence for the ad’s main duty on the CV you actually sent. Move or rewrite that bullet. |
| Recruiter calls end before the technical round. | Practise a one-minute explanation of the work you want and one relevant result. Confirm salary, office expectations and the work situations that require French on the call. |
| Technical rounds keep stalling on the same topic. | Recreate the problem with a small example. Talk a peer through your first check, what it ruled out, and why you took the next step. |
